AirDraw

Paint with your hand in mid-air, in a browser tab. The demo is easy. The engineering was making noisy 30 fps landmark data feel like a pen — steady when you're careful, responsive when you're fast, and unbroken when a frame goes missing.

The problem

Hand-tracking demos are everywhere and almost all of them fall apart the moment a real person uses them. Gestures stop registering as soon as the hand rotates. Strokes shatter into fragments when the tracker misses a frame. Lines wobble because raw landmark coordinates jitter by a few pixels every frame even when your hand is perfectly still. And smoothing that away introduces lag, which feels worse than the jitter did.

None of that is a machine-learning problem — the model is good. It's a signal-processing and interaction-design problem sitting on top of a good model, and that's where all the work went.

Making it feel like a pen

Decision 01

Joint angles, not fingertip heights

The naive way to detect "index finger up" is to compare the fingertip's y-coordinate to the knuckle's. It works beautifully in testing, because in testing your hand is upright and at a fixed distance. Rotate your wrist and it collapses. Lean toward the camera and the thresholds drift.

AirDraw instead computes the angle at each finger joint and normalises against palm size. That makes detection rotation-invariant and distance-adaptive: the same gesture reads correctly whether the hand is upright, tilted, near or far. It's more maths for the same feature — and it's the difference between a demo and something usable.

Decision 02

One-Euro smoothing, because a fixed filter has to pick a side

Landmark coordinates jitter constantly. A fixed low-pass filter forces an unwinnable choice: smooth enough to kill the jitter and every fast stroke lags behind your hand, or responsive enough to keep up and slow careful lines shimmer.

The One-Euro filter adapts its cutoff to pointer speed — heavy smoothing when you're moving slowly and precision matters, light smoothing when you're moving fast and latency matters. Stroke width is modulated by velocity on top of that, which is what gives quick strokes their taper and makes the output read as ink rather than a mouse trail.

Decision 03

Presence smoothing — survive the dropped frame

The tracker occasionally returns nothing for a frame or two: a blur, a bad angle, a moment of poor light. Treated literally, that ends the stroke and starts a new one — so a single continuous gesture comes out as three broken pieces.

AirDraw holds the last known landmark for 180 ms before accepting that the hand is really gone. That window is long enough to bridge one or two dropped frames and short enough that lifting your hand still feels immediate.

Decision 04

The toolbar always wins

An interface that silently reinterprets your intent is infuriating. So gesture recognition never overrides an explicit choice: there are four modes — Auto (index draws, V erases, gesture decides per stroke), Draw, Erase and Pause — and in the three locked modes the toolbar is authoritative no matter what the hand does.

Pause exists for a specific human reason: you want to show someone the canvas, or move your hands while you talk, without smearing what you just made.

Decision 05

Destructive gestures need a different trigger shape

Cycling the palette fires on the rising edge of a thumbs-up, once per pose — otherwise holding the gesture for half a second would strobe through every colour. Clearing the canvas, which cannot be casually undone, requires an open palm held for roughly 600 ms. The trigger mechanism is chosen by consequence, not for consistency's sake.

Decision 06

Never re-render React on a video frame

Drawing runs on a bitmap canvas with destination-out compositing for erase, so rendering cost stays O(1) no matter how long the drawing gets — no growing vector list to redraw each frame. Just as important, per-frame state was moved out of React entirely: routing 30 landmark updates a second through component state was the single biggest source of stutter, and eliminating it did more for perceived smoothness than any filter tuning.

The unglamorous half

A camera-based app has more ways to fail before it starts than after. Permission denied, permission dismissed, no camera present, the model failing to download, an unsupported browser — each of those is a distinct state that needs its own honest message, not a spinner that never resolves.

  • Every failure mode says what happened and what to do — permission denial, model-load failure and unsupported browsers are handled as first-class states rather than as an error boundary.
  • A five-gesture vocabulary, deliberately. An earlier pinch-to-resize gesture was cut rather than kept: it conflicted with the draw pose often enough to be worse than the feature was worth. Removing it made the remaining four more reliable.
  • Brush size and colour are also reachable from the toolbar, so nothing is only available through a gesture — the interface never traps you behind a pose your hand won't hold.
  • Mobile is supported as best-effort and labelled as such — viewport handling, reachable controls, canvas sizing and touch were done properly, while acknowledging that a laptop webcam at desk distance is the setup the gesture tuning targets.
  • The privacy property is structural. There is no backend, so the webcam feed cannot be uploaded — not as a policy, but as an absence of capability.
Honest note

This is genuinely camera-dependent software. It wants reasonable lighting and a hand inside the frame at roughly desk distance; in a dim room, tracking quality drops and no amount of filtering fixes that. The app tells you when it has lost your hand rather than pretending otherwise.
